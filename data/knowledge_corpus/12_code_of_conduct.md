# TechNova Solutions — Code of Conduct
## Purpose
The Code of Conduct establishes ethical standards for employees, contractors, and other representatives of TechNova.

## Integrity
Employees must act honestly, accurately represent business information, and avoid knowingly misleading customers, suppliers, colleagues, or regulators.

## Conflicts of Interest
Employees must disclose situations in which personal interests could influence business decisions. Examples include financial interests in suppliers, close personal relationships affecting procurement decisions, or outside activities that interfere with company responsibilities.

## Gifts and Hospitality
Employees must not offer or accept gifts or hospitality that could improperly influence a business decision. Customer or supplier meals and events must be reasonable, business-related, lawful, and consistent with company rules.

## Anti-Bribery
TechNova prohibits bribery and improper payments. Employees must not provide money, favors, or other benefits to obtain an improper business advantage.

## Respectful Workplace
Employees must treat others with dignity and respect. Harassment, discrimination, threats, and retaliation are prohibited.

## Confidentiality
Employees must protect confidential company, customer, employee, and supplier information.

## Accurate Records
Business records must be accurate and complete. Employees must not create false invoices, manipulate records, or conceal material information.

## Reporting Concerns
Employees should report suspected misconduct through their manager, HR, Ethics channel, or another approved reporting mechanism. Reports made in good faith are protected from retaliation.

## Investigations
Employees are expected to cooperate honestly with authorized investigations and preserve relevant records.

## Enforcement
Violations may lead to disciplinary action up to and including termination, subject to applicable law and company procedures.

## Examples
A supplier offering an expensive personal gift during a contract decision creates a conflict concern and should be disclosed. A manager pressuring an employee to hide an error would violate expectations of integrity.

The Code of Conduct should be considered whenever an employee is unsure whether an action is ethically appropriate.

## Decision-Making
Employees should consider whether an action is lawful, consistent with company values, fair to stakeholders, and something they would be comfortable explaining openly. Pressure from a customer, supplier, or manager does not justify unethical conduct.

## Conflicts During Procurement
Employees involved in supplier selection should disclose relationships or interests that could affect impartiality. The employee may need to step away from the decision depending on the circumstances.

## Records and Expenses
Expense reports, invoices, approvals, and business records must accurately reflect what occurred. Employees must not alter records to conceal personal expenses or bypass controls.

## Confidential Information
Employees who leave the company remain subject to confidentiality obligations that survive employment where applicable. Confidential information must not be taken to a new employer or shared externally.

## Reporting Channels
Employees may raise concerns through their manager, HR, Ethics channel, or another approved reporting mechanism. If a manager is involved in the concern, employees should use an alternative reporting channel.

## Non-Retaliation
Good-faith reporting and participation in investigations should not result in retaliation. Concerns about retaliation should themselves be reported.

## Practical Guidance
When an employee is uncertain, seeking advice before acting is encouraged. Asking for guidance does not automatically imply wrongdoing; it is a responsible way to handle ambiguity.

## Governance and Escalation
Employees should use the designated company system or process for formal requests, approvals, and records. Informal conversations can help coordinate work, but they do not replace required approval or documentation. When a situation does not clearly fit the policy, employees should pause before making a commitment and consult the responsible function such as HR, Finance, Procurement, IT, Security, Privacy, Legal, or their manager.

Managers are responsible for applying policies consistently, documenting material exceptions, and escalating unusual situations rather than creating undocumented local rules. Employees should provide enough context for the reviewing function to understand the business purpose, urgency, affected people or systems, and relevant risks.

Policies may be updated as business, legal, security, or operational requirements change. Employees should use the current approved version of the relevant policy when making a decision. Local law and employment agreements may impose additional requirements and should be followed where applicable.
