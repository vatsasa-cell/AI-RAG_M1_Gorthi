# TechNova Solutions — Employee Onboarding Guide
## Purpose
This guide describes the normal onboarding journey for new employees.

## Before Day One
HR creates the employee record and provides required employment documentation. The hiring manager confirms role, team, start date, and initial responsibilities. IT prepares approved equipment and baseline access according to the role.

## Day One
The employee completes required HR and compliance activities, receives company equipment where applicable, and meets the manager and team. The manager should explain team priorities, communication channels, working hours, and immediate expectations.

## First Week
The employee should complete mandatory security, privacy, acceptable-use, and other required training. Role-specific system access is provisioned after appropriate approvals.

## First Thirty Days
The manager should establish initial goals and confirm the employee understands key processes. The employee should identify training needs and begin contributing to assigned work.

## Access
Access is role-based and follows least privilege. An employee should not request broad access simply because another team member has it. Access requests require business justification and appropriate approval.

## Equipment
Company equipment must be protected and used according to policy. Loss or theft must be reported promptly.

## Remote Employees
Remote employees must follow the Remote Work Policy and use approved secure access methods. Working from an unapproved country is not permitted without required approval.

## Training
Mandatory training normally includes information security, data privacy, acceptable use, and code-of-conduct topics. Additional training may be required for specific roles.

## Manager Checklist
Managers should introduce the employee to key stakeholders, explain team processes, confirm access, set initial goals, and schedule early check-ins.

## Employee Checklist
Employees should complete required documentation and training, protect credentials, ask questions, and communicate blockers early.

## Completion
Onboarding is considered complete when required HR, security, access, and role-specific activities have been completed. The manager remains responsible for ensuring the employee has sufficient support beyond the formal onboarding period.

## First-Week Priorities
The first week should balance compliance with productive integration. Employees should complete required training, verify access to essential systems, learn team communication practices, and understand who to contact for support.

## Access Problems
If required access is missing, the employee should raise an access request through the approved process rather than borrowing another person's credentials. Managers should help prioritize critical access needs.

## Security Expectations
New employees should understand phishing reporting, password protection, approved software, confidential-information handling, and remote-work requirements before handling sensitive company information.

## Role Clarity
Managers should explain the employee's responsibilities, success measures, decision rights, and key stakeholders. Clear expectations reduce delays and unnecessary access requests.

## Equipment Issues
Employees should report damaged, missing, or malfunctioning equipment through IT. They should not attempt unauthorized repairs or install unapproved software to work around an issue.

## Thirty- and Ninety-Day Check-ins
Managers should review progress, blockers, training needs, and goals during early check-ins. These conversations can identify gaps before they become performance problems.

## Practical Guidance
Successful onboarding is complete when required administrative, security, access, and role-specific activities are finished and the employee can work safely and effectively. Support should continue after the formal checklist is completed.

## Governance and Escalation
Employees should use the designated company system or process for formal requests, approvals, and records. Informal conversations can help coordinate work, but they do not replace required approval or documentation. When a situation does not clearly fit the policy, employees should pause before making a commitment and consult the responsible function such as HR, Finance, Procurement, IT, Security, Privacy, Legal, or their manager.

Managers are responsible for applying policies consistently, documenting material exceptions, and escalating unusual situations rather than creating undocumented local rules. Employees should provide enough context for the reviewing function to understand the business purpose, urgency, affected people or systems, and relevant risks.

Policies may be updated as business, legal, security, or operational requirements change. Employees should use the current approved version of the relevant policy when making a decision. Local law and employment agreements may impose additional requirements and should be followed where applicable.
